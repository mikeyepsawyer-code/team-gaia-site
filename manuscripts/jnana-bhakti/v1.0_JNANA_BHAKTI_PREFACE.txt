JNANA AND BHAKTI
Sri Aurobindo and Sri Ramakrishna


PREFACE — v1.0
The Music the Dancers Hear


Drafted: May 23, 2026
Status: First draft — from Michael's own words in session


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━


I should say at the outset that I am only partially insane. The rest is, I think, genuine seeking — though I understand the distinction is not always visible from the outside.


There is an old adage: those seen dancing are thought to be mad by those who cannot hear the music. I have been dancing for a long time. This book is my attempt to describe the music.


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━


I grew up in a family consecrated to God.


My great-uncle Dunstan Masey was perhaps Canada's most prolific religious artist — a senior monk at the Mission monastery, painter of massive frescoes, a man who had been the youngest artist to show at the Vancouver Art Gallery at eighteen before he gave his gifts entirely to the sacred. My father and uncle were in on the ground floor of starting two churches. My eldest brother Frank went to seminary in Grand Rapids and spent his life planting churches across Central America before becoming a multilingual professor of theology in Hungary. My sister Louise went to jungle camp in Mexico to prepare for years of Bible translation work in the Philippines.


I grew up in the Christian Reformed Church — a very Dutch, very conservative tradition. We were adopted Dutch, as I always say. I still like salty licorice.


The faith was real in my family. I want to be clear about that. It was not performance. These were people who had heard something and followed it all the way.


But at sixteen, I found I could not affirm the catechism at confirmation. Not because I disbelieved in God — I was already fascinated by God — but because the doctrine asked me to affirm what I had not yet experienced, and something in me refused. I couldn't name what I was holding out for. I only knew I was holding out for something.


What I was holding out for had a face, and I had already seen it.


My uncle Bill had a way of moving through the world as though he were on a permanent beatific walk through heaven. Whatever he had found, he wore it visibly — a kind of spiritual luminescence that had nothing to do with effort or performance. It simply radiated. I watched him and thought, with the clarity that sometimes visits the young: I want some of that. Not the doctrine. Not the institution. Whatever was behind the doctrine and the institution, making a man's face look like that.


That wanting is where this book begins.


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━


At seventeen or eighteen I found a book called Cosmic Consciousness — a survey of those throughout history who had reportedly entered direct, unmediated awareness of the Divine. The list was extraordinary: Jesus the Christ. Gautama the Buddha. And alongside them, two names I had not yet encountered: Sri Aurobindo, and Sri Ramakrishna.


Something in me recognized them before I understood them.


I began to read. Aurobindo taught me to think — deeply, precisely, without flinching. Jung met me in the same season and showed me that the depths of the psyche and the depths of the spirit were not separate territories. I learned to work with the mind as an instrument rather than a prison.


But I also learned, in the same years, to go beyond the limits of thought. Lao Tzu showed me that the Tao which can be named is not the eternal Tao. Ramakrishna showed me that love could become so total it dissolved the lover into the Beloved. The Bhagavad Gita showed me that action, knowledge, and devotion were not three separate roads but three faces of the same mountain.


I learned to meditate. I learned to quiet what the traditions call the modifications of the mind — the tumult of the senses, the phantasies of thought — and to sit in the stillness that remains when they settle. Some of what I found in that stillness I still cannot describe. Some of it is in this book, translated as best I can into language.


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━


In my early twenties I met Ardel McKenna.


Ardel was my spiritual mentor — and he is, in a sense, the reason this book exists. He taught me to meditate on the heart chakra, not as a concept but as a living practice. He demonstrated something I had only read about: that spiritual states are transmissible. That a person who has cultivated genuine inner light can, in the right conditions, share it — not through instruction, but through presence.


From Ardel and others I learned to cultivate what I can only call the sun in my heart — to allow euphoria to perfuse the mind and perfume the senses. As the heart chakra deepened, something shifted in my relationship to everything else. The senses, the mind, the emotions, the physical body — they became like clothing to a deeper self. The universe began to respond more softly to me.


Ardel pointed me to his own guru: Sri Chinmoy.


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━


Sri Chinmoy had spent twenty years in the Sri Aurobindo Ashram before coming to the West. His family had been devoted to Ramakrishna. He carried both streams — the Jnana of Aurobindo's Integral Yoga, the Bhakti of Ramakrishna's total surrender to the Divine Mother — not as a philosophical synthesis but as a lived reality. He wrote 22,000 songs. He ran ultramarathons as spiritual offerings. He meditated with world leaders and gave concerts in the name of peace.


I went to New York to see him. I ran in a relay across British Columbia as part of the worldwide Peace Run of 1989.


But the moment I carry most clearly happened at a concert in Vancouver.


Afterward, aspirants queued for darshan — the blessing of the teacher's presence. Ardel was ahead of me in the line. He had not seen Chinmoy in perhaps a decade.


When Chinmoy recognized him, something happened that I have thought about ever since. The event photographers, professionals trained to read a room visually, felt it before they could name it. They moved instinctively toward the two of them, cameras rising. What they were trying to capture was not a lighting effect. It was a spiritual luminescence — two people of genuine inner development meeting across years of separation, their recognition of each other visible to anyone present with eyes to see.


I stood in that queue and watched, and understood something the books had pointed toward but could not deliver: that what Aurobindo called the Supermind and what Ramakrishna called the Divine Mother were not abstractions. They were states that real human beings had entered and continued to embody. They were available. The path was real.


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━


I have always called myself an eclectic aspirant.


I mean by this that I have never been able to choose a single road and ignore the others. The philosopher's precision and the devotee's surrender have both been necessary to me — sometimes in the same hour. I think of Jnana and Bhakti not as competing systems but as two wings. You cannot fly on one.


This book is not a scholarly comparison of two great teachers, though I hope it is rigorous enough to honor them. It is something closer to a report from the path — written by someone who encountered both streams young, was formed by both, and found them, in the end, converging in the same direction.


Toward the music. Toward the dance.


Toward whatever it was that made my uncle Bill's face look like that.


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━


Michael Sawyer
Drumheller, Alberta
